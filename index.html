Nicklas Nidhögg

Independent researcher, Sweden

Master of Science in Engineering, Computer Science and Engineering, Lund University

Automata theory, formal languages, lexing and tokenization, parallel scanning

I work on where lexers and tokenizers can safely cut text. A lexer that scans its input in parallel splits it into pieces, and each piece needs a trustworthy starting point: a byte that always begins a token, or, where no single byte does, a short window of text that settles where the token began. I show how to find such points from the token set alone, both for classical maximal-munch lexers and for language-model tokenizers such as byte-pair encoding and WordPiece. The same points tell a lexer where to resume after an error, and anchor incremental editing and auditing. For byte-pair encoding, I determine, for any dictionary, the ripple effect when text is prepended on the left: how far into the text the tokens can change, and when there is no limit.

Where to begin

  • Readers new to the subject can begin with Certified Split Points, which presents the idea in its simplest form.
  • For the theory, Finitely Verifiable Segmentation gives the general framework, and The Left Ripple of Byte-Pair Encoding answers two published open questions.
  • For building tokenizers, the library matisse is a tiktoken-compatible tokenizer that encodes in parallel and as a stream with ids identical to tiktoken's. The Python package matisse-tokenizer is published on PyPI and installs with: 
    pip install matisse-tokenizer
